Rajaraja Chola I (reigned c. 985–1014 CE) rebuilt Chola power from Thanjavur and reshaped South Indian history. He expanded territories into Sri Lanka, the Maldives, and parts of the Deccan, combining land campaigns with naval strength.

His reign is famous for the Brihadeeswarar Temple at Thanjavur, a monumental Shiva temple whose inscriptions document an organized state with detailed revenue districts (kottams), military regiments, and temple servants.

Rajaraja patronized the compilation or preservation of Tamil devotional poetry. The Chola court supported Saiva and Vaishnava traditions while asserting royal ritual centrality.

Administrative inscriptions show measurement of land, irrigation management, and endowments for lamps, musicians, and dancers. His title “Mummudi Chola” reflects claims over three crowns—Chera, Chola, Pandya—expressed in poetry and ritual more than permanent conquest.

Rajaraja’s legacy is imperial but also cultural: bronze casting, temple dance traditions, and Tamil temple towns as nodes of trade and learning. He remains a reference point for Tamil pride in architecture and maritime ambition.
