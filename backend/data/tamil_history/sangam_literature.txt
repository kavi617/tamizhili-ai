Sangam Literature (Tamil: சங்க இலக்கியம்) is the earliest large body of secular Tamil poetry, composed between roughly 300 BCE and 300 CE, though some scholars debate exact dates. The name “Sangam” refers to assemblies of poets patronized by Tamil kings, especially the Pandyas at Madurai.

The corpus is divided into two main groups: Ettuthokai (Eight Anthologies), including Akananuru and Purananuru, and Pattuppattu (Ten Idylls), including works such as Tirumurugarruppadai and Porunararruppadai. Poems are short, often two to thirty lines, but densely crafted.

Two broad themes dominate: akam (interior, love) and puram (exterior, war, public life). Akam poetry uses rich natural imagery—kurinji (mountain), mullai (forest), marutham (farmland), neydal (seashore), and palai (dry land)—to symbolize stages of love. Puram poems praise kings, battles, and generosity at heroic death rites (virakkal).

Sangam poets name rivers, cities, and clans with precision, making the texts vital sources for early Tamil history and geography. They mention trade with Yavanas (foreigners), Buddhist and Jain communities, and diverse occupations from farmers to warriors.

Grammar for classical Tamil was later codified in Tolkappiyam, often studied alongside Sangam texts. The ethical ideal of aram (virtue) appears in the didactic work Tirukkural, sometimes grouped with Sangam-era wisdom literature in popular education.

Sangam poetry values clarity, meter (venba and others), and restraint. It established standards of Tamil prosody and vocabulary that influenced all later Tamil writing. Today these works are taught in schools and remain a foundation of Tamil literary identity and historical imagination.
